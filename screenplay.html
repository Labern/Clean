LABERN · AN ANNOTATED COPY · ONE PAGE ≈ ONE MINUTE
Courier, 12 point — not nostalgia, arithmetic. Ten characters to the inch, six lines to the inch, fifty-five lines to the page: one page of script ≈ one minute of screen time. Hold that. Everything else follows from it.
HOW TO READ A SCREENPLAY
A spec title page holds a title, a byline, a contact — nothing else. No draft number, no date, no epigraph. Bare is confident.

1.
FADE IN:
The starting pistol. A script opens with FADE IN and ends with FADE OUT, and everything between the two is screen time. Nothing else is in the movie.
INT. YOUR ROOM - NIGHT
A slugline: INT. or EXT., the place, the time of day. Every new slugline is a cut — and a company move. Count them and you can price the film: few means a play, many means a chase.
A LAMP. A chair. You. In your lap: a hundred and ten pages of Courier, punched three times, held together by two brass brads. This page is one of them.
Caps are the writer grabbing your sleeve — a character’s first entrance, a crucial sound, a prop that will matter. Capitalized on page one, fired by page ninety.
You turn it over. It makes the sound paper makes when a movie is about to start.
Action lines run in present tense, always: film only has now. Read them at the speed they’d play on screen, not the speed you skim.
YOU
The name hangs 3.7 inches from the left edge; dialogue sits in its own narrow column at 2.5 — a typewriter’s grid, so your eye falls straight down the page at the speed of speech.
(reading aloud)
A parenthetical — actors call them wrylies. A whispered note on delivery. The best writers almost never spend one, so when they do, it’s law.
One page. One minute.
You check the number in the corner. It checks out.
YOU (CONT'D)
Same speaker on both sides of an interruption: the script says (CONT’D) so nobody loses count. Ignore it; it’s bookkeeping.
So this isn't a document. It's a clock.
You read the black parts. You hear the white parts.
YOU (CONT'D)
And nobody's going to explain the rules to me?

V.O. is a voice from outside the scene; O.S. is someone in it you can’t see. Readers who track who’s in the room read twice as fast.
Worse. We're going to explain them while you read.
The lamp BUZZES once. It's in on it.
CUT TO:
Transitions live on the right margin. Mostly ceremony now — every slugline already cuts. But SMASH CUT TO: is a drumroll, and MATCH CUT TO: is a rhyme.
2.
INT. YOUR ROOM - LATER
You do something no novel allows: you fan the pages like a flipbook, reading nothing.
The flip test. Screenwriters design in white space: dense black paragraphs play slow, white pages play fast. You can read a script’s pacing from a meter away, before you read a word.
Pages of dense black. Then pages of nearly nothing.
YOU
I can see where it speeds up.
THE MARGINS (V.O.)
You can see the cutting. Now find page twenty-five.
You do. Something has just gone wrong for somebody.
THE MARGINS (V.O.) (CONT'D)
A script is the only literature where you always know how far you are from the end. Writers build against the number: pages 1–10 earn the read, ~25 breaks the world, ~55 locks the door, ~90 sends the bill.
It always has. Page fifty-five: no way back. Around ninety: everything owed comes due.
INSERT - THE PAGE NUMBER, top right corner, patient as a metronome.
INSERT cuts to a detail — a note, a headline, a trigger — and BACK TO SCENE returns. It’s the writer briefly steering your eyeball: the closest a script comes to a close-up.
BACK TO SCENE.
Then the strangest thing on the shelf: two columns of dialogue standing side by side, like an argument drawn to scale.
YOU
Wait. Both at once? How do I --
THE MARGINS
Both at once. Some writers score talk like music.
Dual dialogue: two people talking over each other, printed as columns. Greta Gerwig scores whole scenes of LITTLE WOMEN this way — overlap on the page becomes family in the room.
3.
INT. YOUR ROOM - LATER STILL
You do not get up for coffee. The movie would not stop for it either.
Two tricks in one line. Read a script in one sitting, at the speed of speech — it was engineered for a two-hour trance. And underlining is the one emphasis Courier owns, a survivor from the typewriter: when a script underlines, hit the word with your eyes.
Outside, a car IDLES. It has been idling since page one.
Everything on a page must be shootable, so everything on the page is a purchase. The writer left your room undescribed and spent a whole line on that car — twice now. Trust the spending: nothing is decoration. If it idles, it arrives.
YOU
(to the script)
Just say what you mean.
THE MARGINS (V.O.)
Nobody in a movie says what they mean. Watch what they do while they say something else.
Dialogue is negotiation, not information. When a character answers a question exactly, be suspicious — flat honesty in a script is usually a lie about something else.
YOU
Are you at least on my side?
THE MARGINS (V.O.)
We are in your lap.
Beat.
A beat is screenwriting’s rest note — a held breath, not an instruction. When a script writes “Beat.”, give it one before you read on.
YOU
That's not what I asked.
You storm to the window. You do not walk angrily. Nobody in a movie has ever walked angrily.
Watch the verbs. Amateurs decorate with adverbs; professionals spend verbs. “Walks angrily” is a note to nobody — “storms” is a shot. In a good script every verb is load-bearing.
Below, the car sits. Idling. Patient.
CUT TO:
4.
INT. THE SHELF - ETERNAL NIGHT
A long shelf of produced screenplays. Every trick on it was written by someone who could already see the film.
The corridor is empty.
A sound.
Steel, settling.
The staccato page: one-line paragraphs, each line its own shot. Walter Hill’s rewrite of ALIEN made this famous — a line break is a cut, and writers who direct edit the film on the page, using your eye as the splicer.
We drift down the shelf. We are in no hurry. We have never been in a hurry.
“We see. We hear. We drift.” The forbidden camera, smuggled in as a pronoun. The manuals ban it; the Coen brothers built a career on it — every sentence has a point of view, and the reader stands exactly where the camera will.
A TITLE CARD:
"CHAPTER FOUR: THE MARGINS STRIKE BACK"
Tarantino writes chapters because chapters are a reader’s pleasure — he writes the read first and trusts the audience to feel the shape. His scripts also call their needle-drops by name; the soundtrack is already playing on the page.
Nothing happens for a while. It is magnificent.
Paul Thomas Anderson will hold a master for pages; THERE WILL BE BLOOD runs nearly fifteen minutes before its first line of dialogue. On the page that’s acres of silent white — writers who direct trust silence, because they’ll be in the room when it plays.
The SPOON circles the TEACUP. Slowly. The shelf goes very quiet.
Jordan Peele writes horror in sound: in GET OUT the capitalized props are the score. When an object earns caps twice, it isn’t a prop anymore — it’s a trigger, and the reader flinches at typography.
And yes -- this is the kind of shelf you'll own too, if this page does its job.
Shane Black talks straight to the reader mid-scene (“the kind of house I’ll buy if this movie is a hit”). Illegal, charming, undefeated — he directs the reading. That’s the secret of all of them: the page is the first cut.

5.
INT. YOUR ROOM - DAWN
The script is finished. You are not. The lamp is still on. Outside, the car has arrived.
THE MARGINS (V.O.)
CHINATOWN for structure. NETWORK for speeches. FARGO for deadpan. ALIEN for silence. PULP FICTION for music. GET OUT for sound.
All of these circulate freely online as PDFs. Read the script first, then watch the film with the pages in your lap — the gap between the two is called directing, and you will never unsee it.
YOU
Tonight?
THE MARGINS (V.O.)
Real time. Lamp on. One page, one minute.
You pick up a pencil. You have margins of your own now.
The last trick: read with a pencil. The people who read scripts for a living argue with them in the margins — the way this copy argued with you. An annotated script is a conversation; a clean one is just a clock that hasn’t started.
